# The CEO and CTO Partnership

**Outcome:** a written working agreement with the CEO, a rhythm that removes surprises, and a board update the CEO has seen before the board does.

## Why this comes first

Practitioner guides on first-time CTO mistakes keep returning to the same two points. A CTO who sees the job as mainly technical misses that every C-level role is a business role, and a CTO who treats the engineering team as the primary team neglects the executive team, which is where company-wide conditions are set. The same guides list tying strategy to business goals and communicating with non-technical stakeholders among the core competencies. This playbook turns those points into practice.

## Six practices

1. **Write the agreement.** Use the [operating agreement template](https://github.com/leanpreneur/cto-os/blob/main/templates/ceo-cto-operating-agreement.md) in the first two weeks. Unwritten expectations drift.
2. **Hold a weekly one-on-one and protect it.** Use it to pre-wire decisions before they reach the wider team, and to raise concerns early with data.
3. **No surprises.** Nothing in a staff meeting or a board meeting should be the first time the CEO hears it from you.
4. **Disagree before the decision, commit after it.** Bring data, say what you see and what you advise, then execute as if the decision were yours. Never undermine a decision in front of your team. They learn from you whether decisions are negotiable.
5. **Translate technical work into business terms.** Lead with the decision needed and the business effect.
6. **Report the same way every time.** A fixed structure makes trends visible and builds trust. Use the [board update template](https://github.com/leanpreneur/cto-os/blob/main/templates/board-update.md).

Four trust foundations sit under these practices: competence (you deliver), candor (you tell the truth early), commitment (you execute decisions fully) and context (you and the CEO share the same picture of the business).

## Translate technical work into business terms

| You are about to say | Say instead |
| --- | --- |
| "We have technical debt." | "Our delivery capacity is reduced by a known share of engineering time. Fixing the top three causes returns that time to the roadmap." |
| "We had an outage." | "Customers were unable to [action] for [duration]. Contractual exposure is [amount or none]. We changed [thing] so it does not repeat." |
| "We need to migrate." | "Staying costs [amount or risk] per quarter. Moving costs [amount] once and removes [constraint]." |
| "Security needs investment." | "Without [control] we cannot pass [customer or regulator requirement], which blocks [deals or markets]." |
| "The architecture will not scale." | "At [customer or volume level], cost to serve grows faster than revenue unless we change [component]." |
| "We need more engineers." | "To deliver [commitment] by [date] we need [capacity]. Today we have [capacity]. The gap is [number], and the options are [cut scope, move date, add people]." |

Fill every bracket with a real number or write "unknown, measured by [date]". A translated sentence without numbers is still jargon.

## Deliver bad news

1. Tell the CEO before the problem spreads, in person or by phone, not by email alone.
2. Lead with the conclusion: what happened, what it affects, what you are doing, what you need.
3. Give the business effect first and the technical cause second, and only in depth if asked.
4. Bring an option and a recommendation, not only the problem.
5. Follow up in writing the same day.

A short script:

> "I need five minutes. [Conclusion in one sentence.] It affects [customers, revenue, date]. We are [action] and will update you at [time]. I recommend [option]. I need [decision or resource] from you by [time]."

## Disagree well

1. Raise the concern privately and early, before the decision is made.
2. State the evidence, the risk and your recommendation.
3. Let the CEO decide. If the decision stands, say "understood, I will make it work" and do.
4. Record the decision with the [decision record](https://github.com/leanpreneur/cto-os/blob/main/templates/decision-record.md) when it is architectural or organizational.
5. Revisit only when new evidence arrives, and say so: "This is new information."

## Prepare for the board

| When | Action |
| --- | --- |
| 14 days before | Pull the metrics. Mark gaps as Unknown. Draft the headline and the risk table. |
| 10 days before | Meet the CEO. Agree the three messages and the asks. |
| 7 days before | Pre-wire any director who will need context, with the CEO's agreement. |
| 3 days before | Send the full draft to the CEO. Incorporate edits. |
| Day of | Present the headline in two minutes, then take questions. End with the asks. |
| 1 day after | Send written follow-up on commitments, decisions and open questions. |

What boards ask about is performance, cost and risk. Frame each section as an investment or a risk, and report with the same structure each quarter so the board can see trends. Pair every metric with a target or benchmark, a green, amber or red status and, for any amber or red, the planned response. Give conservative forecasts alongside targets. Close with specific asks.

## Common failures

| Failure | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| The CEO learns of a problem from the board or a customer | Apply the no-surprises rule. Send a short heads-up the day you know. |
| Reports describe activity (tickets closed) instead of outcomes | Report customer, delivery, cost and risk results. |
| Jargon | Run the translation table on every slide. |
| Defensiveness when challenged | Ask what decision the challenge supports and answer that. |
| Hiding risk to look in control | Report risks with owners and trends. A visible risk with a plan builds more trust than a clean page. |
| Re-arguing a settled decision | Record it. Reopen only on new evidence. |

## Definition of done

- A signed operating agreement dated within the first 30 days.
- A weekly one-on-one on both calendars.
- A first board update in the standard structure, seen by the CEO three days before the board.
- A translation table in your own words for your five most common messages.

## How you will know it worked

- Leading: the CEO has no unfamiliar item in any staff or board meeting, and the weekly one-on-one has not been cancelled twice in a row.
- Lagging: board questions shift from "what happened" to "what are the trade-offs," and the CEO asks for your input on decisions outside engineering.
